Insights Brief

Industry & Company Analysis

Financial Performance

Unite Students had an exceptional 2025 with a significant increase in earnings by 15% to £144.2 million (Allen, 2025), while achieving a 7.4% in rental growth. However, they face a threat in property yields, with total accounting returns dropping from 7.9% to 4.0%.

A major reason for dropping yields is the rise in fixed utility bills, which represent the second largest share of expenses with an 13% annual increase to £30.5 million (Biggs, 2025). At the same time, the certainty of fixed income also became volatile, specifically in short-term summer rentals. Older assets become uninhabitable since the number of properties at risk of overheating due to utility abuse will triple from 44 to 128 by 2050, risking an extra £15 million of income per year, while potential flooding damage costs are also projected to reach £37.8 million.

If they fail to digitally transform their utilities and infrastructure, Unite risks facing a brown discount (Corlett, 2022), referring to properties that are “not energy-efficient, sustainable, or environmentally compliant”, dampening the value of their properties and the lending rates they charge.

Customer Trends

Strong cost sensitivity increasingly shapes how students evaluate sustainability. Within the private landlord rental market, 58% of students check sustainability features before moving in, with energy-efficient insulation (15.2%) and smart controls for lighting and heating (12.9%) ranking among the most important decision factors. In general, rising energy prices have further increased cost-consciousness, with 69% of students wanting greater control over their energy consumption (Knight Frank, 2025).

Currently, 85% of students prioritise “bills included” for budget certainty and reduced flatmate conflict. However, this bundling often incentivises excessive usage as students seek to maximise perceived value. Crucially, research shows students are most likely to adopt sustainable behaviours when real-time feedback is paired with visible monetary rewards, such as vouchers (Ivanova et al., 2025), presenting a clear opportunity for Unite to align operational efficiency, sustainability goals, and student experience through smart energy management and engagement.

Competitive Landscape

UK PBSA market’s valuation is strong in 2024, totalling £3.87 billion with over 76,000 beds to support growth driven by short-term occupancy levels. Unite continues to lead all peers operationally over competitors such as iQ and GSA. However, iQ has the Most Valuable London Portfolio, while also having a greater overall amount of its assets rated EPC A or B, 98.0% vs. 91.7% for Unite (Merrick, 2025). Also, GSA has recently engaged Measurabl to automate its sustainability data management system and has reduced its emissions by 3,201 metric tonnes of CO2 (Team, 2025).

Currently, Unite’s energy intensity is lagging regarding its scheduled trajectory because of an increase in the use of student bedrooms and their legacy systems. While competitors are adopting monitoring systems based on artificial intelligence to provide them with energy savings, Unite continues to rely on human supervision. This creates a significant risk for Unite to miss its EBIT margin target of 50 basis points, which will have a substantial effect on its profitability.

Emerging Tech in the Sector

The UK private PBSA market grew by 76.9% since 2014/15 (Statista, 2025). Private PBSA currently has 55.92% market share, a dominance stemming from the effects of the Renters’ Rights Bill on the property market, leading to the decline of traditional HMOs.

Price sensitivity is a major affordability constraint as 77% of students are domestic (Bolton et al, 2025). In London, the average annual PBSA rent of £13,595 almost equates the £13,762 maximum maintenance loan, creating a significant funding gap (HEPI, 2024). To compete with proprietary residence, PBSA pricing models need to become more flexible, moving away from all-inclusive models where tenants overpay for energy wastage. Creating digital dashboards to track consumption would allow the creation of “saver plans”, which would appeal to 76% of students who put affordability first, whilst also maintaining a high rate of occupancy, as seen by incumbents such as Unite Group and Watkin Jones, with 56% (Allen, 2025) and 51% (Giddins, 2025) of university nominations respectively.

For Unite Students, we propose deploying EcoSync’s smart TRV and cloud-based heating control platform to cut energy waste while enhancing digital oversight of building performance (EcoSync, 2025). EcoSync’s retrofit TRVs allow Unite to cap temperature ranges, automatically turn off heating in void or persistently under-occupied rooms, and use AI-driven optimisation to reduce gas consumption and emissions in line with Unite’s ESG targets. Real-world case studies such as St Edward’s School, which achieved one-third reductions in heating demand through EcoSync, demonstrating the potential applicability of this technology to Unite’s buildings.

Unite can draw confidence from the PBSA-specific results already delivered by Prefect Controls’ Irus system at Bangor University’s Ffriddoedd Village, a large student village where centralised digital heating management has been successfully rolled out at scale, showing that a networked, data-driven control layer is both technically and operationally feasible in UK student accommodation.

Critique of Mapify Output

One limitation of the information on the mindmap is the lack of geographic context. Our research treats the UK PBSA market as a single entity, rather than specifically dividing the areas in terms of demand or prices. The mindmap lacks information from other major cities such as Manchester and Bristol, which arguably are facing a more severe housing shortage, or in the southern areas of England, where there is housing oversupply. Those specific circumstances can significantly influence the utility costs of accommodation and the rents that Unite can charge.

Similarly, even in the same geographical area and even the same property, Unite offers various room types from shared rooms, en-suites to studios at drastically different price points. No data is found regarding the specific demand for room types, limiting the specific applicability of the mindmap.

Another limitation is how the information generalises all domestic students as price-sensitive, ignoring the different circumstances of students coming from different parts of the UK. For instance, a student moving from London to smaller cities like Exeter will likely be more willing to accept overpriced properties since they are still by far cheaper than properties in London. There is also a lack of consideration of the price sensitivity of international students who typically are from more wealthy backgrounds.

Furthermore, the information neglects broader macroeconomic trends. In 2025, the interest rates remain higher than pre-pandemic levels, and there is heightened uncertainty in the economic policies due to the geopolitical threats by Trump in the form of tariffs, potentially contributing to a decrease in the “Total Accounting Returns”. Similarly, the rise in “utility costs” could simply be caused by the UK’s commitment to import more expensive energy from the US instead of alternatives like OPEC. The change in immigration policies, for example, the consistent rise of maintenance requirements for student visas, could also contribute to the decrease in fixed income for Unite.

Some minor issues include how most data are long-term projections and predictions. For instance, the number of properties at risk of overheating will triple to 128 by 2050, yet they could easily be subjected to change due to further regulation or shifts in climate.

Lastly, the examples of small-scale institutions deploying EcoSync, like St. Edward’s School, limit external validity and may not be generalisable to implementation across the country, where there would be far more students and denser living spaces.
